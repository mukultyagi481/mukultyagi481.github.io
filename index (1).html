Forecasting demand when the economy is part of the signal

The best-fitting model was the worst forecaster. Selecting on backtest error instead of R² cut forecast error from 16.5% to 13.9% MAPE — and only that model beat "same month last year."

Methods
SARIMAX with exogenous drivers, AIC order selection, all-subsets regression, rolling-origin backtesting
Data
Synthetic monthly demand and macroeconomic series matching the original problem's structure
Tools
Python, statsmodels, SQL, Tableau, Power BI
Context
Based on a team capstone with Raymond Corporation (Toyota Industries), Jan–Jun 2025
Links
Code and memo

The question

Demand for industrial trucks swings with the economy. If we forecast the macroeconomic drivers forward and feed them into a demand model, do we get a forecast the business can plan against — and does it beat assuming next year looks like last year?

What I did

  • I built the two-stage pipeline: SARIMAX forecasts of interest rates, consumer confidence, CPI, and retail trade volume, then a demand regression on trend, product class, and those drivers.
  • I compared all 15 driver subsets under rolling-origin backtesting, three folds of twelve months each, using only prior history at every step.
  • I added a seasonal naive benchmark, and scored each model twice — with drivers forecast and with drivers known — to separate the two sources of error.

What I found

Choosing the specification with the highest in-sample R² selected all four drivers (R² = 0.972) and produced 16.5% MAPE out of sample, worse than the 14.4% you get from simply repeating last year. Choosing on backtest error selected a single driver, the 10-year rate, at 13.9%. Splitting the error explains why: with drivers known, the four-driver model forecasts to 6.4% MAPE, so the demand regression is genuinely good — but forecasting consumer confidence and CPI twelve months out introduces more error than those variables remove. A driver only earns its place if it can be forecast about as accurately as the thing you're predicting, and in-sample R² cannot see that.

What I'd do next

Use a VAR for stage one so the drivers move together rather than being forecast independently, propagate stage-one uncertainty into prediction intervals instead of shipping point forecasts, and treat consumer confidence as a short-horizon nowcast rather than a twelve-month projection.

How much faster can an A/B test finish?

Using pre-experiment data cut the sample needed for 80% power by 26%. Daily peeking raised the false positive rate from 5% to 22%.

The question

Engagement metrics are skewed, so tests on small changes need a lot of traffic. We already know how each member behaved before the test began. How much of that noise can we remove, and what does checking the results every day cost?

What I did

  • I simulated a two-arm test on lognormal watch time, where a member's pre-period behaviour correlates 0.8 with their in-experiment behaviour.
  • I compared difference in means, CUPED, and OLS regression adjustment on bias, 95% interval coverage, and power across six sample sizes, 400 replications each.
  • I ran a separate study with no true effect, monitoring daily for 14 days and stopping at the first significant result.

What I found

All three estimators were unbiased with coverage at 95–96%, so the variance reduction does not distort inference. CUPED reached 80% power at 53,400 users where difference in means needed 72,200, a 26% reduction. CUPED and regression adjustment agreed to three decimals, which is expected with a single covariate. Peeking mattered more than the choice of estimator: with no real effect at all, the daily-monitored test declared significance 22% of the time.

What I'd do next

Add a predicted-engagement covariate for members with no history, since they gain nothing from CUPED, and compare an alpha-spending sequential design against the fixed-horizon test.

Measuring a rollout you could not randomize

Churn rose 0.33 percentage points in treated regions, confirmed by a synthetic control and 18 placebo tests.

Methods
Two-way fixed effects DiD, event study, synthetic control, in-space placebos
Data
Simulated 40-region, 36-month panel; accepts a real panel via a flag
Tools
Python, statsmodels, SciPy
Links
Code and memo

The question

A price increase goes out to some regions and not others, and cancellations rise afterwards. How much of that rise was the price change, and how much was seasonality and everything else happening at the same time?

What I did

  • I estimated the effect with region and month fixed effects, standard errors clustered by region.
  • I ran an event study to test whether pre-rollout trends were actually parallel.
  • I built a synthetic control from untreated regions with non-negative weights summing to one, then repeated the whole procedure pretending each untreated region was treated.

What I found

DiD gave +0.33 pp [0.25, 0.41] and the synthetic control +0.37 pp, against a true effect of +0.40 pp. Pre-rollout event study coefficients sat near zero, weights spread across 14 donor regions with none above 0.21, and none of the 18 well-fitted placebos produced a gap as large as the real one, for a pseudo p-value of 0.053.

What I'd do next

Handle staggered rollouts properly, where two-way fixed effects is biased, using a Callaway–Sant'Anna estimator, and weigh the churn cost against revenue per retained member.

Choosing a metric you can still defend in six months

Days active needs a 0.65% lift to be detectable where watch hours need 2.30%, and predicts retention better.

Data
Simulated cohort of 40,000 sign-ups with 90-day outcomes
Tools
Python, lifelines, scikit-learn
Links
Code and memo

The question

Teams pick a primary metric on intuition. A metric is only useful if it is sensitive enough to move in a test of realistic size and predictive of whether the member is still subscribed months later. Which candidate clears both bars?

What I did

  • I scored five early metrics on the smallest lift detectable at 80% power with 50,000 members per arm.
  • I scored the same metrics on how well they separate members who stay from members who leave at 90 days.
  • I modelled time-to-cancel with Kaplan–Meier curves by device and a Cox model on early behaviour and acquisition channel.

What I found

Days active in the first 14 days won on both axes: a 0.65% detectable lift and an AUC of 0.724 for 90-day retention, with a 54 percentage point retention gap between top and bottom quintiles. Watch hours sound closest to member value but their heavy tail makes them the second-noisiest candidate. Survival at 90 days ranged from 61% for TV-first members to 50% for mobile-first.

What I'd do next

Pair the metric with guardrails against gaming, since days active can be inflated by notifications, and validate with a long-horizon holdout that the causal link to retention actually holds.

Deciding what is worth A/B testing

Item-to-item recommendations beat popularity by 12.8% on NDCG@10 and covered 15 times more of the catalogue.

Data

Tools
Python, SciPy sparse, NumPy
Links

The question

Online tests are expensive and offline metrics can mislead. Given three candidate rankers, which one earns a slot in the test queue, and what test would settle it?

What I did

  • I split by a global time cutoff so no model saw the future, and evaluated 3,140 users on NDCG@10, Recall@10, and catalogue coverage.
  • I bootstrapped over users for intervals on every metric, and broke results down by how much history each user had.
  • I wrote the online test the result implies: primary metric, secondary metrics, guardrails, assignment, duration, and decision rule.

What I found

Item-to-item cosine won with non-overlapping intervals. Untuned ALS lost to the popularity baseline on ranking quality while covering the most catalogue, a reminder that the more sophisticated model is not automatically better. The gain was largest for users with short histories and shrank for the heaviest users, whose future views are already close to the popular set.

What I'd do next

Tune ALS properly before declaring a winner, and rerun the whole pipeline on MovieLens 25M to see whether the ordering survives on real data.

Can you beat “assume no change” on a real, volatile series?

Across 78 backtest origins, no model beat the random walk by a statistically detectable margin. The honest twelve-month planning number is about ±45%.

Methods
Expanding-window backtesting, MASE, Diebold–Mariano tests, Holt–Winters ETS, SARIMA with AIC selection
Data
Real: 354 months of US natural gas prices (EIA) deflated by CPI-U (BLS), 1997–2026
Tools
Python, statsmodels, SciPy
Links
Code and memo

The question

Forecasts usually get judged against each other and almost never against the trivial alternative. I took a real input-cost series that feeds operating expense planning and asked which models beat the naive benchmarks, at which horizons, and whether the difference is distinguishable from luck.

What I did

  • I pulled thirty years of published price data, deflated it to constant dollars, and scored four models over 78 expanding-window origins at horizons of 1, 3, 6, and 12 months.
  • I used MASE so errors are comparable across horizons, and ran a paired Diebold–Mariano test of every model against the random walk rather than eyeballing the averages.

What I found

The random walk won at every horizon, and the p-values against it ranged from 0.06 to 0.23 — the gaps are consistent with noise. Seasonal naive was significantly worse at short horizons (p < 0.005): gas demand is seasonal, but the price level is dominated by a random-walk component, so forecasting last winter's price loses to forecasting last month's. I reported the result as it came out rather than tuning until a model won.

What this means for a planning decision

Quote input-cost forecasts as ranges, not points. Don't let in-sample fit justify replacing a naive benchmark. Spend the effort on hedging and scenario planning instead of squeezing a better point forecast out of a near-random walk. A model that can't beat the benchmark still tells the business where not to spend.

What I'd do next

Add storage levels, weather, and production as exogenous drivers — the most likely route to actually beating the benchmark — and switch to an asymmetric loss function, since over- and under-forecasting cost different amounts.

Six business questions, answered in SQL

Rock leads every market, but Latin is 27.6% of Brazilian revenue and the Czech Republic's next two genres aren't music at all. A global promotion list would be wrong almost everywhere.

Tools

The question

Most analytics work is a sequence of SQL questions, not a model. I wrote the six queries an analyst would actually run against a transactional schema, in the order the analysis proceeds: trend, cohorts, segmentation, concentration, lapse risk, then the checks that decide whether the numbers can be reported at all.

What I did

  • I built monthly revenue with month-over-month, year-over-year, and a trailing three-month average, so one noisy month doesn't read as a trend.
  • I built first-purchase cohorts by quarter and tracked each as a share of its original size, because blended retention hides acquisition-mix changes.
  • I ranked genres within each country partition, with a revenue floor so tiny markets don't produce confident nonsense.
  • I measured each customer's normal gap between orders and flagged who is overdue against their own baseline, instead of one global churn window.
  • I wrote a data-quality query that reconciles invoice totals against line items and checks for orphan keys, returning one row per check as a pass/fail report.

What I found

Beyond the market-level taste differences, revenue turned out almost perfectly uniform across value deciles — the top decile holds 12% where a real consumer business usually shows 40% or more. That is a property of this generated dataset rather than a finding, and catching it before anyone builds a retention strategy on it is the point. I flagged it in the memo instead of presenting it as an insight.

What I'd do next

Port the same queries to a real warehouse where the quality checks actually fail, and add an incremental build so the cohort table doesn't rebuild from scratch each run.
